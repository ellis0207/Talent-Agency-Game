# Talent Agency Game: Grand Plan

> Working title: **Talent Agency Game** (placeholder; final name TBD)
> Status: **v1.0**, written with Ellis from three rounds of Q&A. This is a living document; update it as decisions change.

---

## 1. Vision

You start as a lone agent with a desk, a phone and a few hundred dollars. You end as the head of a talent empire whose clients headline stadiums, win championships and star in blockbusters.

The game is about **spotting potential before anyone else, shaping careers, and winning at the negotiating table.** Every client is a person with ambitions, quirks and a shelf life. Your job is to turn them into stars and take your cut.

**Pillars**

1. **The eye for talent.** Scouting is a skill. Hidden potential, partial information, and gambles on unknowns are the heart of the game.
2. **Careers are stories.** Each client has a personality, goals, relationships and an arc that unfolds through events and choices.
3. **The deal is the boss fight.** Negotiations are tense, readable, and reward preparation and leverage.
4. **Build the machine.** Grow from a one-person shop to a multi-division agency with staff, offices and influence.

## 2. Platform, Tone & Presentation

- **Platform:** Desktop, released on Steam. Mouse and keyboard first; controller support is a stretch goal.
- **Feel:** A management game driven by characters, story and choices. It must **never feel like a spreadsheet**. Numbers exist, but the player reads them through people, faces, headlines and consequences rather than tables.
- **Tone:** Mostly grounded, with a satirical, humorous edge. The entertainment and sports industries are fair game for parody: egos, PR spin, tabloid scandals, and the occasional over-the-top disaster.
- **Art style:** 2D cartoon. Reference feel: *News Tower* (chunky, readable, characterful, a living building you can look into). Ours is inspired by that warmth and readability, not a copy of its look.
- **Business model:** Passion project for now, built so a premium Steam release stays possible. That means:
  - All leagues, teams, labels, studios, brands, venues and people are **fictional**. No real athletes, celebrities, logos or trademarked league names.
  - Parody targets archetypes, not identifiable real individuals.
  - All art, music, fonts and code dependencies must have licenses that allow commercial use.

### Design rule: "faces, not figures"

Wherever a spreadsheet would show a number, we first try to show a person, a reaction, or an object in the world. Examples: a client's morale is their portrait's expression and what they say when you walk by; an offer's value is shown as a headline and a comparison ("more than your last three deals combined"); stats appear only when the player hovers or drills in.

## 3. Core Loop

The player is **the agency itself**, a faceless institution like the newspaper in *News Tower*. There is no player avatar. You see the agency through its building, its staff and its clients.

### The week

Time moves in **week-by-week turns**. Each week has three beats:

1. **Plan (Monday morning).** Review the inbox: offers, client requests, scout reports, rival moves, headlines. Assign staff to tasks (scouting trips, negotiations, PR campaigns, legal cases). Decide where each client's career should go next.
2. **Play out the week.** Time runs. The building comes alive: agents on phones, clients dropping by, scouts returning from trips. **Events interrupt** and demand a decision: a client is caught on camera, a rival is courting your star, a studio wants an answer by Friday.
3. **Results (Sunday night).** Games, gigs, premieres and shoots resolve. Commissions come in, reputations shift, headlines summarize the week. The player sees consequences as stories first ("Your striker scored twice and is now trending in Brazil"), numbers second.

### The longer loop

Weeks roll into **seasons** (each sport and industry has its own calendar: drafts, free agency, award seasons, festival circuits, fashion weeks). Over seasons the player:

**Scout → Sign → Develop → Deal → Grow the agency → Unlock bigger talent and markets → Repeat at a higher tier.**

## 4. Talent

### Launch divisions

| Division | Examples of careers | Where the money comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **Athletes** | Many sports, each with its own league rules (see §5) | Playing contracts, endorsements, appearance fees |
| **Musicians** | Solo artists, bands, producers, DJs | Record deals, touring, publishing, sync licensing, brand deals |
| **Actors** | Film, TV, theater, voice | Role fees, backend points, series regular deals, endorsements |
| **Models** | Runway, commercial, editorial, fitness | Campaigns, runway bookings, exclusive brand contracts |

**Unlockable divisions later** (candidates): comedians, influencers/streamers, esports players, authors, chefs, directors, coaches. Each is unlocked through agency growth and campaign choices.

### Everyone starts small

The agency begins with **low-tier talent only**: college walk-ons, lower-league players, bar bands, background actors, catalogue models. Stars won't return your calls until your reputation earns it. The thrill is finding the unknown who becomes a legend.

### What a client is

Every client is a character, not a stat line.

- **Visible:** portrait (2D cartoon, generated from parts), name, age, origin, current tier, mood, and a short "one-liner" personality tag ("Golden boy with a temper").
- **Revealed through scouting:** skill, **hidden potential** (a range that narrows the more you scout), work ethic, injury proneness, marketability.
- **Personality traits:** e.g. Loyal, Greedy, Diva, Party Animal, Perfectionist, Homebody, Media Darling, Hothead. Traits drive events and how clients react to your advice.
- **Ambitions:** what they personally want (a ring, a Grammy, to play at home, money for family, fame, artistic credibility). Fulfilling ambitions builds loyalty; ignoring them breeds resentment.
- **Relationships:** with you, with other clients (rivalries, romances, collaborations), with teams, labels and directors.
- **Will of their own.** Clients can refuse advice, demand a deal, go rogue on social media, fire you, or be poached. Trust is the core resource you manage with each client.

### International talent

Talent comes from all over a fictional-but-recognizable world (real countries and regions are fine; real people, teams and brands are not).

Country and region matter:

- **Talent pools:** some regions produce more of certain sports or genres, and scouting there is cheaper once you have a presence.
- **Rules and paperwork:** work visas, import or foreign-player limits in certain leagues, international transfer rules, tax differences.
- **Fame is local first:** a client can be huge at home and unknown abroad. Breaking a star into a new market is a big career move.
- **Language and culture:** affect adjustment, morale and media relations when clients move abroad; translators and local staff help.
- **Regional offices:** the agency can open branches abroad to scout and serve local clients (see §6).

## 5. Careers & Contracts

### Your toolkit for each client

Deals, career direction, public image, advice, PR and legal assistance. Concretely:

- **Career direction:** pick goals ("move to a contender", "go indie", "pivot to TV").
- **Deals:** negotiate contracts, endorsements and bookings.
- **Image & PR:** shape their public persona, run campaigns, manage crises.
- **Advice:** weigh in on personal choices (which offer to take, who to date publicly, whether to retire). They may ignore you.
- **Legal:** contract disputes, lawsuits, scandals, holdouts, morality clauses.

### Negotiation: the boss fight

Negotiations are a scene, not a form. You sit across the table from a character (a general manager, label exec, casting director or brand rep) with their own personality and goals.

- **Leverage** comes from your client's form, rival offers, media buzz, your agency's reputation and timing (deadlines, free agency, award buzz).
- **Moves:** make an offer, counter, bluff about other interest, walk away, call in a favor, leak to the press, bundle clients.
- **Read the room:** the other side's mood and patience are shown through their face and dialogue. Push too hard and they walk; too soft and you leave money on the table.
- **Every deal has terms that matter later:** length, guarantees, options, incentives, image rights, release clauses, morality clauses, backend points.

### Real-world rules, fictional names

Sports contracts follow the **real rules of the league they mirror**, close enough to feel authentic without getting pedantic. League names, teams and logos are fictional. Game mechanics and rule structures are not protected by copyright, so this is safe as long as names and branding are original.

Examples of rule sets to model (each a "ruleset module" per fictional league):

| Fictional league mirrors | Key rules to model |
|---|---|
| US pro basketball | Salary cap with exceptions, max and minimum contracts tied to years of service, rookie scale for draft picks, Bird-rights-style re-signing advantage, restricted vs unrestricted free agency, two-way and 10-day style deals |
| US pro football | Hard cap, rookie wage scale, franchise-tag-style tags, guaranteed vs non-guaranteed money, signing bonuses |
| US pro baseball | No hard cap with a luxury tax, arbitration years, service time, minor league system, international amateur signing pools |
| US pro hockey | Hard cap, entry-level contracts, RFA offer sheets, arbitration |
| European football (soccer) | Transfer fees and windows, release clauses, loans, free transfers at contract end, homegrown quotas, work-permit style rules |
| Combat sports | Fight-by-fight purses, promotion exclusivity, PPV points, title shots |
| Individual sports (tennis, golf, athletics) | Prize money, rankings, appearance fees, sponsorship-driven income |
| Others to consider | Cricket leagues, motorsport seats, cycling teams, rugby, college/amateur eligibility rules |

Each league ships with its own **season calendar** (draft, trade deadline, free agency, playoffs) that creates natural moments of tension.

Entertainment divisions get equivalent rules: record deal structures (advances, recoupment, royalty rates, 360 deals), film/TV deals (quotes, backend, option periods, union minimums), and modeling exclusives and usage rights.

### Career arcs

Clients rise, peak and decline. Athletes age and get injured; musicians chase hits and reinventions; actors get typecast or break out. Late-career choices (retire on top, one last contract, transition to coaching, broadcasting or producing) are emotional story beats, and retired clients can become staff or future connections.

## 6. Agency Growth & Progression

### The building

The agency is a **visible, buildable building** you look into from the side, in the spirit of *News Tower*. You start with one cramped room and expand floor by floor.

- **Rooms:** agent offices, scouting department, PR suite, legal office, recording booth, gym/training room, casting studio, photo studio, lounge, conference room for negotiations, executive floor.
- Rooms unlock services and boost related work. Decor and comfort affect staff morale and how impressed visiting talent is during a pitch.
- The building is alive: staff working, clients visiting, chaos during a scandal, a party after a big signing.

### Staff

Since the player is faceless, **staff are the agency's hands**. Each is a character with skills, specialties (a sport, a region, a genre), personality and ambition.

- **Agents** handle clients and negotiations. **Scouts** find talent. **PR people** manage image. **Lawyers** handle disputes. **Assistants**, **analysts**, **translators** and more.
- Staff can be poached by rivals, can go rogue and start their own agency, or can rise to become division heads.

### Reputation and tiers

Reputation is tracked per division and per region. It gates which talent will talk to you, which execs take your calls and which deals are available. Progression feels like moving up through tiers: from local hustle to regional player to national power to global empire.

### Rivals

Rival agencies are a **major pillar** and a main driver of the campaign story.

- A handful of **named rival agencies**, each led by a memorable character with a distinct style (the ruthless megaagency, the boutique artist-friendly shop, the sleazy upstart, the old-money establishment, the foreign giant expanding into your market).
- Rivals scout, sign, poach your clients and staff, bid against you, leak stories about you and lobby leagues and studios.
- Relationships with rivals can shift: feud, truce, partnership, hostile takeover, merger.
- Rival leaders have their own arcs in the campaign, and how you deal with them shapes the branching story.

## 7. Economy, Risk & Difficulty

### Money

- **Income:** commissions on every deal, in line with real-world norms per industry (roughly 3–4% on team sports playing contracts, 10–20% on endorsements, music, acting and modeling). Commission rates can be negotiated with each client at signing; greedy clients push back.
- **Costs:** staff salaries, rent and building upgrades, scouting trips, PR campaigns, legal fees, travel, client perks, signing bonuses to win talent, fines.
- **Cash flow is the pressure.** Big commissions are lumpy and arrive with deals; costs arrive every week. Early game is a scramble to stay afloat.
- **Bankruptcy ends the run.** The game shows clear warning signs before it happens (late rent, staff complaining, creditors calling) and offers emergency options (sell a client's contract to a rival, take a predatory loan, downsize).

### Playing clean or dirty: risk and reward

Many problems can be solved cleanly or dirtily. Dirty tactics pay more or work faster, but carry **risk**.

- **Examples:** leaking a story to create leverage, tampering with another agency's client, burying a scandal, inflating a client's stats or buzz, bending league rules, poaching staff with secrets, bribing a gossip columnist.
- **Every tactic shows its odds and stakes before you commit** (as a readable gamble, not a spreadsheet: "Good chance this works. If it backfires, expect a league investigation.").
- **Staff and equipment shift the odds.** A fixer, a well-connected lawyer, a PR wizard, a security team or a better-equipped office each improve specific tactics. Rival agencies with better people are harder to outplay.
- **Consequences:** fines, league bans, lawsuits, lost client trust, damaged reputation, rival retaliation and tabloid storms.
- **Reputation has two axes:** how powerful you are and how clean you are. A shady agency can thrive, but some clients, leagues and brands won't work with it, while others prefer it. Both paths are viable.

### Difficulty

Chosen at the start (and fully customizable in sandbox). Settings:

| Setting | What it changes |
|---|---|
| **Risk forgiveness** | Odds and severity of dirty tactics backfiring |
| **Negotiation toughness** | How hard the other side bargains and how quickly they walk |
| **Rival aggression** | How often rivals poach, bid against you and attack your reputation |
| **Client volatility** | How often clients rebel, demand things or need rescuing |
| **Economy** | Starting money, costs and how close bankruptcy runs |

Presets: **Intern** (forgiving), **Agent** (standard), **Shark** (punishing), plus Custom.

## 8. Game Modes

- **Campaign.** **No long backstory: you hop straight in.** A short cold open ("Your agency opens Monday. You have one desk, a phone and enough cash for a month.") and you're playing. The early weeks are the tutorial, taught through real tasks (sign your first client, close your first deal, survive your first crisis). The campaign has a clear arc toward becoming the top agency, driven by **scripted story characters** (rival bosses, a legendary free agent, a league commissioner, a tabloid editor) whose arcs **branch** based on your choices: which divisions and regions you grow into, which rivals you crush or ally with, and whether you play clean or dirty. Different paths lead to different later chapters and endings. Beating the campaign lets the player **continue the same save as a sandbox**.
- **Sandbox.** Start fresh with custom settings (starting money, unlocked divisions and regions, difficulty sliders). No ending; play as long as you like.

### Procedural world

Clients, staff, teams, labels, studios, brands and execs are **procedurally generated**: names drawn per country, portraits assembled from cartoon parts, traits, potential and ambitions rolled with sensible distributions. The scripted campaign characters live alongside them. A seed system lets players replay or share a world.

## 9. Technology

- **Engine: Godot 4** (recommended and chosen for now).
  - Free and open source (MIT license) with no royalties, so there's no licensing cost or legal risk for a premium Steam release.
  - Excellent for 2D and UI-heavy games, which is exactly this game. Unreal is powerful but built for 3D and is heavier than we need for a 2D cartoon management game; it also carries a revenue share above its threshold.
  - Code and scenes are plain text files, which works well for the two of us building it together in Git, and Godot can run headless for automated tests.
  - Steam export and Steamworks integration are supported through the community GodotSteam plugin.
- **Language:** GDScript for game code. Simulation rules (contracts, league rulesets, negotiation, events) are kept as data plus pure logic so they can be unit tested.
- **Data-driven content:** leagues, rulesets, traits, events, names and dialogue live in data files (JSON or Godot resources), so adding a sport or event means adding data, not rewriting code.
- **Art pipeline:** placeholder art first. Cartoon portraits built from layered 2D parts. Blender can be used for pre-rendering building pieces if useful, but the game itself is 2D. All assets must be original or commercially licensed.
- **Testing:** automated tests for the simulation, using a small in-repo test runner (`tests/`) that works in the editor, on the command line and on GitHub. We can move to GUT (a Godot unit test framework) if the tests outgrow it. Every phase ends with a build Ellis can run.
- **Setup:** step-by-step setup for Godot and tools is in [`docs/SETUP.md`](SETUP.md). The project targets **Godot 4.7** (standard edition).

## 10. Roadmap

Built in **phases**. Every phase ends with something **testable**: a build Ellis can run and play.

| Phase | Goal | What you can test |
|---|---|---|
| **0. Foundations** | Godot project, repo structure, test setup, data formats, placeholder art style | A window that opens, a test suite that runs, a fake week that advances |
| **1. The first week** | Weekly turn loop, inbox, one division (basketball), sign a low-tier client, one simple negotiation, money in and out | Sign a player, negotiate his first deal, advance weeks, watch money change, go bankrupt |
| **2. Living clients** | Client personalities, traits, ambitions, mood and trust; random events that interrupt; advice and refusals | Clients that react, demand, rebel and leave |
| **3. Real rules** | Full basketball ruleset (cap, max/min, rookie scale, free agency, season calendar); negotiation as a full scene | A full season with draft and free agency |
| **4. The building** | Visible side-view building, rooms, staff hiring and assignment | Expand your office floor by floor and watch it come alive |
| **5. Rivals** | Rival agencies that scout, bid, poach and react | Lose a client to a rival, steal one back |
| **6. Risk & reputation** | Clean vs dirty tactics, odds shaped by staff and equipment, scandals, legal and PR tools | Pull off (or botch) a dirty tactic and live with it |
| **7. Entertainment** | Music division (then actors and models), with their own deal structures | Manage a musician alongside athletes |
| **8. More sports & the world** | More leagues and rulesets, international talent, regions, visas, regional offices | Sign a foreign prospect and deal with the paperwork |
| **9. Campaign** | Tutorial onboarding, scripted characters, branching chapters, endings, continue-as-sandbox | Play the campaign start to finish |
| **10. Sandbox & difficulty** | Difficulty presets and sliders, custom starts, seeds | Start a custom sandbox on Shark difficulty |
| **11. Polish & Steam** | Final art, audio, balance, save system hardening, Steam integration, store page | A release candidate |

Phases may be split smaller as we go; the rule is that each one ends playable.

## 11. Legal Checklist (for a premium release)

- No real league names, team names, logos, athlete or celebrity names, or real brand names.
- Real countries and cities are fine; real organizations are not.
- Rule structures can mirror real leagues (mechanics aren't copyrightable); names and wording must be original.
- *News Tower* and other games are inspirations for feel only; no copied art, UI layouts, names or assets.
- Every third-party asset, font, sound and plugin must have a license that allows commercial use, recorded in a credits/licenses file.
